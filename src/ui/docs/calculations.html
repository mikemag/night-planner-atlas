How it works

Atlas finds astronomical darkness, then applies your Moon settings to find usable windows.

Twilight and true dark

Atlas evaluates each night from local noon to the next noon. True dark is the time the Sun’s center is at least 18° below the horizon.

Solar event thresholds

Civil, nautical, and astronomical twilight use geometric Sun-center altitudes of −6°, −12°, and −18°. Sunrise and sunset use −0.833° at sea level to account for the solar radius and standard refraction. Elevation adjusts the refraction term.

The Moon and usable windows

Strict moon-free time is true dark with the Moon’s upper edge below the natural horizon. The height setting raises that cutoff. The crescent setting accepts all true dark when the Moon’s illumination at local midnight is below the chosen percentage.

Atlas adds the elapsed time in each separate window and drops pieces under one minute. Displayed times and totals round independently.

A night with two windows

On June 29–30, 2026, the Moon stayed up throughout true dark at Seattle’s Paramount Park. A 15° cutoff accepts two windows when its upper edge was below that height.

  • 12:17–1:21 AM (1h 4m)
  • 1:46–2:09 AM (23m)

That’s 1h 27m usable, with moonlight in both windows. Times are PDT.

Why rise/set events and altitude curves use different points

Rise and set use the Moon’s apparent upper edge. River’s pointer shows its apparent center, sampled every ten minutes. Separate crossing searches determine the event times.

Elevation and the horizon

Elevation adjusts the observer’s position and refraction in a standard atmosphere. Atlas assumes a level horizon. A raised Moon cutoff applies in every direction, so check it against the actual horizon where you plan to observe.

Lunar cycles and new moons

  • Phase names describe the Moon at local midnight. Several nights can carry the “New moon” label.
  • Calendar marks the actual new moon on its local civil date. River marks the associated observing night, which can have a different date.
  • Lunations groups nights by the new moon closest to each night’s local midnight. Full cycle shows each loaded night once.

Weekend picks rank Friday and Saturday by their combined usable time. Lunations uses the nights shown. River searches Friday starts within seven nights of each new moon, starting from tonight.

Clock changes and polar nights

  • Clock changes. Totals count elapsed time through a skipped or repeated hour.
  • Polar nights. Twilight can occur even when the Sun stays below the horizon. The Sun’s altitude determines how much true dark the night contains.

Accuracy and limits

In the tested U.S. Naval Observatory cases, solar events agreed within 30 seconds and lunar events within 34 seconds. The tables round to the minute. Actual rise and set times depend on refraction and your local horizon.

Search windows, numerical tolerances and reference provenance
  • Solar searches span the full noon-to-noon night. Moon searches usually cover noon +2 h through noon +22 h, extending for longer periods below the horizon.
  • Moon crossings use a ten-minute scan, refined extrema, and bisection to a 50 ms bracket. That tolerance describes the search within the model.
  • Reference fixtures preserve the published values and query URLs for Seattle, Edinburgh, and Sydney. Separate cases cover polar conditions and clock changes. The lunar crossing and elevation audits use the same underlying ephemeris.
