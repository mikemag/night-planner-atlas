How it works

The same Sun and Moon calculations power every view. Here are the definitions, assumptions and checks behind them.

Twilight and true dark

A night runs from local noon to the next local noon. As the Sun drops below the horizon, the sky passes through civil, nautical and astronomical twilight. True dark begins when the Sun’s center is 18° below the horizon.

DayCivilNauticalAstronomicalTrue dark

Solar events are found at −0.833° for sunrise/sunset at sea level, and −6°, −12° and −18° for twilight. Elevation adjusts idealized refraction at sunrise and sunset; twilight uses the geometric Sun center.

The Moon and usable windows

Strict moon-free time is true dark with the Moon’s upper edge below the natural horizon. Usable dark applies your planning choices: a higher Moon threshold, or acceptance of a thin crescent, can add moonlit time.

The Moon-height choice uses the apparent upper edge at 0°, 5°, 10° or 15°. Ignore a Moon under 10%, 20% or 30% lit accepts all true dark when illumination at site-local midnight is below that choice. These controls change windows and totals; new-moon focus changes the presentation.

The drawing always shows moonlight above the natural horizon. Ordinary moonlight has a warm grey wash; accepted moonlight has a green wash in Light/Dark and an additional dashed cue in Observing. Truly moon-free time stays black. The wash illustrates illumination, rather than calibrated sky brightness.

Separate windows stay separate. Totals add elapsed time from every piece, discarding pieces shorter than one minute. Times and durations are rounded independently for display.

Why rise/set events and altitude curves use different points

Moonrise is the moment the Moon’s upper edge reaches a level horizon as seen from your site. The calculation includes lunar parallax, a radius that changes with distance and atmospheric refraction. The Moon’s center can still be below 0° when its upper edge appears.

River’s pointer altitude shows the apparent center, sampled every ten minutes. The samples describe position; event times and usable-window boundaries come from separate refined crossing searches.

Natural-horizon Moon events use 34 arcminutes of sea-level refraction. The elevated Moon thresholds use altitude-dependent refraction; elevation scales refraction with air density.

Sunrise and sunset use a Sun-center altitude of −0.833° at sea level, representing the conventional visible-disk edge with standard refraction. Twilight uses the geometric Sun center at −6°, −12° and −18°. See the USNO definitions of rise, set and twilight.

A real night with two windows

At Seattle’s Paramount Park, Monday June 29 → Tuesday June 30, 2026 has true dark from 12:17–2:09 AM on Tuesday. The nearly full Moon is up throughout, giving no strict moon-free time.

With the Moon threshold at 15° and crescent ignoring off, the accepted windows are 12:17–1:21 AM (1h 4m) and 1:46–2:09 AM (23m): 1h 27m in total. The Moon rises above the chosen threshold in the gap. These are two separate accepted sessions, with moonlight in both.

Elevation and the horizon

Optional elevation accepts −500 to 10,000 metres above sea level; blank means sea level. It adjusts the observer position and idealized atmospheric refraction. The horizon stays level: elevation is not a terrain profile, and no horizon dip or local obstruction is inferred.

A Moon-height threshold can stand in for a simple obstruction, but it applies at every azimuth. Check ridges, trees, weather, transparency, light pollution and target visibility separately.

Lunar cycles and new moons

Exact new-moon instants come from the lunar calculation. Illumination and written phase names describe the Moon at site-local midnight; the “New moon” phase name can therefore appear on several nights around one event.

River marks the night associated with the event. Calendar marks its actual site-local civil date. Those can differ because an observing night crosses midnight. Lunations assigns each whole night to the new moon nearest its local midnight, with ties going to the earlier event.

Full cycle includes every loaded night exactly once. Empty alignment positions are padding; partial cycles mean some owned nights lie outside the loaded dates. A focused ±N window intentionally shows fewer nights.

Lunations totals and Friday–Saturday picks use its displayed owned nights. River’s Dark weekends ahead searches Friday starts within ±7 nights of new moon, from tonight onward, and lists up to seven picks. Both rank by combined usable duration, retaining the earlier Friday on a tie.

Clock changes and polar nights

Charts use the site’s local clock time, including daylight saving. Window totals use actual elapsed time through a repeated or skipped hour; a fall-back window can last longer than its clock labels suggest.

At high latitudes, a missing sunrise does not imply 24 hours of true dark. Midday twilight can still occur. All solar stages follow their below-threshold intervals, including continuous daylight, continuous darkness and intervals split at local noon. Missing events remain absent; noon boundaries are never invented rise/set events.

Accuracy, with the comparisons attached

The reference suite uses independently published, minute-rounded USNO annual tables for Seattle, Edinburgh and Sydney. The committed cases were verified on October 16, 2026 and include northern/southern seasons, absent twilight, new/full moons and both directions of a daylight-saving change.

Search windows, numerical tolerances and reference provenance

Solar events are searched from local noon to the next local noon, including clock changes. Moon events and Moon-up intervals ordinarily use noon +2 h through noon +22 h. Longer below-horizon periods extend that window; when sunrise or sunset is absent, it covers the full noon-to-noon period. Events outside the search window are absent. A clipped interval edge is not reported as a rise or set.

Moon crossings are refined at 0°, 5°, 10° and 15°. A ten-minute scan brackets extrema, refined extrema split the altitude curve into monotone arcs, and bisection locates crossings to a 50 ms bracket. This preserves grazing event pairs hidden between samples. A tangent touch adds no Moon-up interval.

The audit checks all 365 nights of 2026 at Seattle, Edinburgh, Sydney, Kjell Henriksen Observatory and an Antarctic site at 85° south at all four heights, using an independent one-minute scan and crossing refinement of the same underlying ephemeris. Synthetic tests cover two-second grazing pairs that a one-minute scan could miss. These isolate numerical search error rather than testing an independent lunar ephemeris.

Elevation checks at −400 m, 2,000 m and 4,500 m compare with Astronomy Engine’s separate level-horizon rise/set search: within one second for the Moon and ten seconds for the Sun. They use the same ephemeris and do not establish independent elevated-site accuracy.

The stored USNO fixture retains exact query URLs, decimal reference coordinates, UTC dates and verbatim table cells. Reference dates are Seattle October 3, 9, 25 and 31; Edinburgh June 20; Sydney October 3 and December 21, all in 2026. UTC tables avoid applying a fixed civil offset across DST. The reference fixture is never regenerated from the planner’s own results.

Additional polar checks compare Kjell Henriksen’s March, June and December 2026 solar events and continuous-daylight/darkness cases with USNO annual tables. Polar night still contains midday twilight there. Separate checks cover uninterrupted true dark nearer the poles, split intervals and 23/25-hour clock-change periods.
